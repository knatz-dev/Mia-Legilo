It was not the journey itself that wore Shevek down, but the continuous weight of expectation. On Urras he had been treated like a sovereign dignitary, a prize mind fetched from an austere wilderness, kept perpetually beneath lamps and gilded cornices until even silence began to feel like luxury stolen from someone else’s vault.

When the shuttle engines cut at last into orbital deceleration, the deep rumble shuddered into the steel floorboards and climbed straight through his ankles into his marrow. He closed his fingers around the worn silver compass Takver had slipped into his coat pocket twenty-eight months prior. The glass had developed a faint hairline crack during the dockside riot in Nio Esseia, but the needle still floated true in its alcohol bath, completely indifferent to political frontiers or treaties signed in dry air.

“There is nothing you can own that does not eventually own you in return,” he muttered into the stale recycled cabin draft, recalling the old Pravic primer they had all memorized in the mountain dormitories at Sedoh. Urras had promised him an abundance so dense it choked the lungs; yet here, suspended between two worlds in the freezing void, he felt no exile in his heart.

Below the curved quartz port, the dusty ocher crescent of Anarres began to swell against the velvet dark. There were no cloud whorls here, no sapphire seas like those that made Urras look like a dripping jewel in an open palm. Only the dry, mineral skin of rock, salt flats, and scrub, waiting for nothing other than those who knew how to ask for water without claiming the spring.

Finis Caput XIII
Chapter Fourteen

On Anarres

T he dust of the landing apron smelled of crushed stone and sulfur, an abrasive, clean sharpness that stung the nostrils before the warm air could even reach the throat. To Shevek, stepping off the gangway into the high-noon glare was not an arrival but an unpeeling. The coat tailored for him by the Council of World Ministers on Urras felt absurdly heavy, woven from sheep’s wool so densely brushed it felt like felted armor.

A man in a faded blue sun-tunic stood waiting at the foot of the mobile stairs. He was squinting into the copper horizon, holding a wooden clipboard strapped with two rusted spring clips. No trumpets, no formal detachment of armed guards in polished leather cuirasses, no journalists with silver lens tubes hovering like bluebottle flies around a fresh cut of meat.

“You are Shevek,” the man said. It was not phrased as an interrogation or an announcement. It was merely a coordination of facts, spoken in the level, unaccented cadence of someone identifying a parcel of seed oats arriving from the North Coast Syndicate.

“I am Shevek,” he answered. His voice sounded dry, like paper scraping against coarse slate. He took a long inhalation of the thin, cold breeze blowing off the Southwest Dunes. “Has Takver left Chiffewar yet?”

The clerk flipped a sheet on the board. “She is at the Abobenon Biological Station. The drought broke in the third decile; they are planting drought-grass in the gullies before the frost settles. She sent a message two days ago by line-post: tell him his desk is where he left it, but the roof leaked twice in the rainy season. She said you would understand what that meant.”

Shevek laughed aloud—a short, rough bark of sound that startled a solitary sand-sparrow perched upon the fuel pipeline nearby. The bird flared its mottled brown wings, hovered for a second against the relentless violet sky, then settled again without fear. On this world there were no birds kept in brass cages, just as there were no locks upon the doors of the storehouses.

He swung his canvas satchel over his shoulder, felt the weight of his manuscripts bump against his hip, and began walking across the sunbaked gravel toward the low earthen dome of the transit dormitories.

Shevek had not spoken for some miles. The dust of the road settled on his wool coat, silvering the heavy grey fabric. Across the dry scrub-plain of the South Anarres tableland, the wind moved like something alive, ancient, without hurry or malice.

"It is not that we have less than the Urras people," Takver said softly, leaning her cheek against the coarse curve of his collar. "It is that we do not have things between us and our lives. Our poverty is real; but our hands are clean."

He took her hand. It was rough-skinned, sun-darkened, familiar as his own breathing. The low lorry rattled down into the hollow where the water-station stood, its silver cistern catching the pale glare of the twin moons rising together in the violet twilight.

Chapter Fourteen

On Anarres

The Institute of Sciences at Abenay had no gates. Its low adobe pavilions spread over the arid rise above the town like sunbaked terraces, unadorned by crests or guards. A student or an elder walked through the open courtyards with the same calm, unhurried stride, their sandals clicking faintly on the clay pavers.

There were no keys because there were no locks. In the communal dining refectory, bread baked from the hard northern grains was stacked in wide wicker hampers beside jugs of fermented herb-tea. Shevek stood in the morning doorway watching the light break across the cinder-cone of Mount Silum.

He had begun the calculations for the General Temporal Theory three years before, while sleeping in a tent at the copper mines. Now, surrounded by chalkboards stained with decades of ghost-equations, the solution seemed both impossibly distant and as immediate as the cold air entering his throat.
